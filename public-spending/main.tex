\begin{abstract}\small\sloppy
\noindent
This paper studies how population aging affects capital accumulation and labor
informality when the government finances a fixed social-security outlay per
older adult. Let $d_t=N_t^o/N_t^y$ denote the ratio of people aged 65 and over
to the working-age population. Government outlays per working-age adult obey
the identity $G_t^{SS}=\bar g_o d_t$. I embed this fiscal block in an annual
deterministic general-equilibrium model in which private capital is used only
in formal production and workers choose between formal and informal
employment. The Colombian illustration raises $d_t$ from 15.02 percent in 2024
to 27.28 percent in 2050, using DANE population projections, while holding
$\bar g_o$ fixed. Initial public pension outlays are normalized to 5.7 percent
of GDP. If broad taxes finance the entire aging-induced increase, stationary
capital and informality do not change and private consumption falls 5.8
percent. If half of the increase is charged to the formal wage bill, capital
falls 13.2 percent and informality rises \MixedInformalityChange. If that
share is 75 percent, capital falls \PayrollCapitalChange{} and informality
rises \PayrollInformalityChange. Financing the entire increase on formal
payroll has no interior stationary equilibrium in this calibration. The model
treats social-security outlays as a real resource claim. It is therefore a
reduced-form aging exercise, not a literal model of pension transfers; that
interpretation requires separate generations and retired-household budgets.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Keywords:} population aging; social security; informality; capital
accumulation; payroll taxation; Colombia.
\quad
\textbf{JEL:} E22; E62; H55; J11; J46.

\section{Introduction}

Population aging creates a fiscal pressure even when policy rules do not
change. If every older adult receives a fixed real amount of pensions, health
care, or long-term care, total social-security outlays rise with the number of
older people relative to the tax-paying population. The relevant primitive is
therefore not an arbitrary path for government purchases. It is the
demographic ratio that maps the number of beneficiaries into a fiscal claim.

This paper studies that mechanism in Colombia. Let $N_t^o$ be the population
aged 65 and over and $N_t^y$ the population aged 15--64. The government assigns
a fixed real outlay $\bar g_o$ to each older adult. After normalizing the model
by the working-age population, social-security spending is
\[
  G_t^{SS}=\bar g_o\frac{N_t^o}{N_t^y}=\bar g_o d_t.
\]
DANE projections imply that $d_t$ rises from 15.02 percent in 2024 to 27.28
percent in 2050 \citep{DANE2025Projections}. Holding $\bar g_o$ fixed converts
that demographic change into a permanent increase in the government's revenue
requirement.

The financing rule determines how the demographic pressure reaches the rest
of the economy. A tax on the formal wage bill lowers the relative return to
formal employment and shrinks its own base. A sufficiently broad tax does not
create that formal--informal wedge in the present model. Public debt could
spread the adjustment over time, but it cannot finance a permanently larger
old-age population without a corresponding long-run primary revenue stream.

The model is annual and deliberately parsimonious. A representative household
chooses private consumption and saving. Formal firms use capital and labor,
whereas informal production is labor intensive. Workers select formal or
informal employment according to after-tax wages and heterogeneous sector
preferences. A smooth discrete-choice margin keeps both sectors active. The
capital stock follows the Euler equation and the aggregate resource constraint.

The initial calibration matches \BenchmarkInformality{} national labor
informality, measured by \citet{DANE2025Informality}, and sets public pension
outlays to \BenchmarkGovernmentShare{} of GDP, the Colombian value reported by
\citet{OECD2023Colombia}. The demographic experiment keeps the outlay per older
adult fixed and replaces the 2024 dependency ratio with DANE's 2050 projection.
The remaining parameters are provisional. The exercise is consequently a
mechanism decomposition, not a forecast of Colombian pension or health
expenditure.

The stationary results separate the resource claim from the formal-employment
wedge. Under broad-tax financing, capital, output, and informality remain at
their initial values; private consumption falls 5.8 percent. If half of the
additional outlay is financed from the formal wage bill, informality rises 5.9
percentage points, capital falls 13.2 percent, and output falls 6.2 percent.
When 75 percent is charged to formal payroll, informality rises 10.2 percentage
points and capital falls 22.9 percent. A rule that places the entire increase
on formal payroll does not admit an interior stationary equilibrium. That last
result is an endogenous fiscal limit, not a numerical convergence failure.

One accounting qualification is essential. Cash pensions are transfers between
households, not government absorption of output. Health and long-term care are
closer to real public consumption. The current representative-household model
treats the entire $G_t^{SS}$ as a real resource claim. This shortcut permits a
transparent analysis of capital and informality, but it cannot describe
intergenerational redistribution or retired households' saving. A full pension
interpretation requires an overlapping-generations extension.

The paper contributes a compact link between demography, fiscal financing, and
informality. The next section places the exercise in the literature. The
remaining sections present the model and calibration, report stationary and
transition results, discuss the conceptual limits, and document the numerical
algorithm.

\section{Related literature}

This paper connects three strands of work. The first studies the fiscal effects
of population aging. For Colombia, \citet{Ospina2024} project the public cost of
Colpensiones and special regimes and show that longevity, growth, and formality
materially change the long-run fiscal outlook. Their exercise models pension
rules and coverage in detail. The present paper takes a narrower route: it
fixes the outlay per older adult and isolates the general-equilibrium response
of capital and informal employment.

The second strand concerns fiscal policy in general equilibrium. In the
neoclassical model of \citet{BaxterKing1993}, persistent government purchases
affect private wealth and investment. \citet{Barro1990} distinguishes
unproductive government consumption from productive public services, while
\citet{Gali2007} shows that household heterogeneity and market structure can
change private-consumption responses. Here the demographic path is exogenous,
but its financing changes the formal labor wedge.

The third strand studies informality. \citet{Levy2008} emphasizes that
payroll-financed social benefits can tax formal employment and alter the
allocation of labor. \citet{LaPortaShleifer2014} and \citet{Ulyssea2018} show
why informal firms cannot be represented only as tax-evading versions of
formal firms. Productivity, technology, firm size, and selection differ across
sectors. The current model retains only the labor-allocation margin and should
be interpreted accordingly.

For Colombia, \citet{MejiaPosada2007} study formal and informal production when
regulation can be evaded and enforcement is costly. \citet{Osorio2016}
examines tax reform and informality in dynamic general equilibrium.
\citet{Rincon2017} embed government consumption, public investment, and a
fiscal rule, while \citet{GrandaMorales2023} analyze taxes, enforcement, and
labor-market rigidities. \citet{Herrera2025} provide a richer Colombian fiscal
model with several revenue sources, expenditure categories, debt, and
sovereign risk.

The contribution here is not comparable institutional detail. It is the
identity $G_t^{SS}=\bar g_o d_t$ combined with an endogenous formal tax base.
That structure makes visible which effects come from aging itself and which
come from the instrument used to finance it.

\section{Model}

\subsection{Household preferences and capital accumulation}

Time is discrete and one period is one year. The representative household
maximizes
\begin{equation}
  U_0=
  \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^t
  \left[
    \frac{C_t^{1-\sigma}-1}{1-\sigma}
    +\chi_G\log G_t^{SS}
  \right],
  \qquad
  \beta\in(0,1),\quad \sigma>0.
  \label{eq:utility}
\end{equation}
Private consumption is $C_t$. The term $G_t^{SS}$ denotes social-security
outlays per working-age adult. This public term is optional and additive.
Because it is exogenous to the household, it does not enter the first-order
condition for private saving:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t}
  =
  \left[\beta(1+r_{t+1})\right]^{1/\sigma}.
  \label{eq:euler}
\end{equation}
Changing $\chi_G$ changes welfare evaluated along an allocation but does not
change the allocation itself.

Private capital evolves according to
\begin{equation}
  K_{t+1}
  =
  (1-\delta)K_t+Y_t-C_t-G_t^{SS}.
  \label{eq:capital}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:capital} treats social-security spending as a real resource
claim. This is natural for publicly provided health and care services. It is a
reduced-form shortcut for cash pensions, which are transfers rather than
aggregate absorption. A literal pension model must assign benefits and taxes
to different generations.

\subsection{Formal and informal production}

Formal firms use private capital and formal labor:
\begin{equation}
  Y_t^F=K_t^\alpha(L_t^F)^{1-\alpha},
  \qquad \alpha\in(0,1).
  \label{eq:formal_production}
\end{equation}
The competitive formal wage and net return on capital are
\begin{align}
  w_t^F
  &=
  (1-\alpha)
  \left(\frac{K_t}{L_t^F}\right)^\alpha,
  \label{eq:formal_wage}
  \\
  r_t
  &=
  \alpha
  \left(\frac{L_t^F}{K_t}\right)^{1-\alpha}
  -\delta.
  \label{eq:return}
\end{align}

Informal output uses labor only:
\begin{equation}
  Y_t^I=A_I L_t^I,
  \qquad
  w_t^I=A_I.
  \label{eq:informal_production}
\end{equation}
The labor force has unit mass, so $L_t^I=1-L_t^F$. Total output is
\begin{equation}
  Y_t=Y_t^F+Y_t^I.
  \label{eq:total_output}
\end{equation}

Equation \eqref{eq:informal_production} is a benchmark, not a claim that
informal firms literally use no equipment. It represents labor-intensive
services and microenterprises with limited access to formal finance. The
assumption also implies that a shift out of formal employment reduces the
labor input used together with private capital. A robustness exercise should
allow informal firms to use a smaller amount of capital and should introduce a
financing wedge rather than assume frictionless mobility of capital across
sectors.

\subsection{Endogenous informality}

Workers differ in their idiosyncratic valuation of the two sectors. Aggregating
extreme-value preference shocks gives the formal employment share
\begin{equation}
  L_t^F
  =
  \Lambda\left(
    \frac{
      \log[(1-\tau_t^F)w_t^F]
      -\log w_t^I-\kappa
    }{\mu}
  \right),
  \qquad
  \Lambda(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}.
  \label{eq:formal_choice}
\end{equation}
The parameter $\kappa$ is the average nonpecuniary cost of formal employment.
The scale $\mu$ controls heterogeneity. A positive $\mu$ prevents the economy
from collapsing mechanically into a corner when after-tax wages change.

The formal payroll tax $\tau_t^F$ affects both the tax base and the allocation
of labor. A higher tax lowers the net formal wage in
\eqref{eq:formal_choice}. The resulting fall in $L_t^F$ reduces the wage bill
on which the tax is collected. The model therefore checks for an interior
fiscal equilibrium rather than calculating the tax rate from a fixed base.

\subsection{Population aging and fiscal closure}

Let $N_t^o$ denote people aged 65 and over and $N_t^y$ people aged 15--64.
Define the old-age dependency ratio
\begin{equation}
  d_t\equiv\frac{N_t^o}{N_t^y}.
  \label{eq:dependency}
\end{equation}
Each older adult requires a fixed real social-security outlay $\bar g_o$.
After normalizing all aggregates by the working-age population, government
spending is
\begin{equation}
  G_t^{SS}=\bar g_o d_t.
  \label{eq:social_spending}
\end{equation}
Demography is exogenous in this version. The government does not choose
$G_t^{SS}$ independently of $d_t$ and cannot reduce the per-capita commitment
$\bar g_o$ along the experiment.

The government budget balances each period:
\begin{equation}
  G_t^{SS}=\tau_t^F w_t^F L_t^F+T_t,
  \label{eq:government_budget}
\end{equation}
where $T_t$ is a broad fiscal instrument represented as lump sum. Let
$G_0^{SS}=\bar g_o d_0$ and
$\Delta G_t^{SS}=G_t^{SS}-G_0^{SS}$. Required payroll revenue satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \tau_t^F w_t^F L_t^F
  =
  \lambda_0G_0^{SS}
  +\lambda_\Delta\Delta G_t^{SS}.
  \label{eq:payroll_finance}
\end{equation}
The remaining revenue is
\begin{equation}
  T_t=(1-\lambda_0)G_0^{SS}
  +(1-\lambda_\Delta)\Delta G_t^{SS}.
  \label{eq:lump_sum_finance}
\end{equation}

All experiments share $\lambda_0$ and the same initial equilibrium. The
broad-tax benchmark sets $\lambda_\Delta=0$, mixed financing sets
$\lambda_\Delta=0.5$, and payroll-heavy financing sets
$\lambda_\Delta=0.75$. The broad instrument is a theoretical benchmark, not a
claim that Colombia can collect nondistortionary taxes. Setting
$\lambda_\Delta=1$ is also evaluated as a feasibility check; under the present
calibration it destroys the interior stationary formal-sector allocation.

\subsection{Equilibrium}

Given a demographic path $\{d_t\}$ and a marginal financing share, an
equilibrium is a sequence
\[
  \{C_t,K_{t+1},L_t^F,\tau_t^F,w_t^F,r_t,Y_t,G_t^{SS}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
\]
that satisfies household optimality \eqref{eq:euler}, capital accumulation
\eqref{eq:capital}, factor prices \eqref{eq:formal_wage}--\eqref{eq:return},
endogenous formality \eqref{eq:formal_choice}, the demographic spending rule
\eqref{eq:social_spending}, and the government budget
\eqref{eq:government_budget}--\eqref{eq:lump_sum_finance}.

In a stationary equilibrium,
\begin{equation}
  r^\star=\frac{1}{\beta}-1
  \label{eq:ss_return}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \frac{K^\star}{L^{F,\star}}
  =
  \left[
    \frac{\alpha}{
      \beta^{-1}-1+\delta
    }
  \right]^{1/(1-\alpha)}.
  \label{eq:ss_capital_labor}
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{eq:formal_choice},
\eqref{eq:payroll_finance}, and
\eqref{eq:ss_capital_labor} jointly determine formal employment, the payroll
tax, and capital.

55.4 percent national labor informality, measured by
\citet{DANE2025Informality}. Public pension spending is set to 5.7 percent of
GDP, as reported for Colombia by \citet{OECD2023Colombia}. This figure fixes
the scale of the fiscal outlay; it does not turn pension transfers into public
consumption in national-accounting terms.

The demographic ratios are calculated from DANE's July 2025 national
population projections by single year of age. The numerator is population aged

per-elderly outlay is calibrated from the initial identity
\[
  \bar g_o=\frac{G_0^{SS}}{d_{2024}}.
\]
It is then held fixed.

For the numerical transition, the demographic comparison is represented as an

\begin{equation}

\section{Results}

\subsection{Stationary effects}

\input{results/steady_state_table.tex}

Table \ref{tab:steady_results} separates the resource effect of aging from the
formal-tax effect. Under the broad-tax benchmark, the formal employment wedge
does not change. The stationary return condition fixes the formal
capital--labor ratio, so capital, output, and informality remain at their
initial values. The larger social-security claim is absorbed by private
consumption, which falls 5.8 percent.

With mixed financing, half of the additional outlay is collected from the
formal wage bill. The payroll tax rises 6.6 percentage points. Informality
increases from 55.4 to 61.3 percent, capital falls 13.2 percent, output falls
6.2 percent, and private consumption falls 11.2 percent. Formal labor and
capital are complements, so the contraction of the formal tax base also lowers
the capital stock.

Payroll-heavy financing places 75 percent of the additional outlay on formal
payroll. The payroll tax rises 11.4 percentage points and informality reaches
65.6 percent. Capital falls 22.9 percent, output 10.8 percent, and private
consumption 15.2 percent. Requiring payroll to finance 100 percent of the
aging-induced increase produces no interior stationary allocation. As the tax
rate rises, formal employment and the wage bill contract too strongly to meet
the revenue requirement. The model therefore signals a fiscal limit rather
than reporting an artificial corner as an equilibrium.

Figure \ref{fig:transition} reports the perfect-foresight transition implied by

The endogenous paths are monotone near the new steady state. The algorithm
selects the stable branch of the Euler and resource equations; it does not
smooth, average, or damp the computed series. Numerical residuals converge at
the terminal allocation.

\subsection{Social spending and welfare}

The allocation is identical whether $\chi_G=0$ or $\chi_G>0$ in
\eqref{eq:utility}; only evaluated welfare changes. With $\chi_G=0$, the
permanent proportional increases in private consumption required to restore
benchmark welfare are 6.1 percent under broad-tax financing, 9.5 percent under
mixed financing, and 11.8 percent under payroll-heavy financing. With
$\chi_G=0.15$, those compensations become $-1.9$, 1.3, and 3.4 percent,
respectively.

The negative number in the broad-tax case does not establish a welfare gain
from Colombian pension spending. It reflects the assumed utility value of an
aggregate that the model also treats as public absorption. A welfare analysis
of pensions must instead value retirees' consumption and distinguish who pays
from who receives.

\section{Extensions and limitations}

The main limitation is the treatment of social-security outlays. Cash pensions
are transfers; health and long-term care absorb real resources. Combining them
in $G_t^{SS}$ is useful for a first mechanism exercise but not for welfare or
national-accounting decomposition. An overlapping-generations model should
assign taxes to workers, benefits and health services to older households, and
allow both groups to save.

Second, $\bar g_o$ is fixed. Actual pension benefits depend on contribution
histories, replacement rules, indexation, and coverage. Health expenditure also
varies sharply within the older population. A quantitative application should
replace the scalar $\bar g_o$ with age-specific and program-specific schedules.

Third, the budget balances every year. Debt could smooth the demographic
transition, but a permanent increase in $d_t$ still requires a long-run primary
adjustment. A debt extension should add an intertemporal government budget and
a terminal fiscal rule rather than use debt as a residual.

Fourth, the broad-tax instrument is represented as lump sum. Observed
consumption and income taxes have their own behavioral margins. The current
comparison therefore identifies the additional distortion created by formal
payroll financing, not an optimal tax mix.

Fifth, informal production uses labor only and formal and informal output are
perfect substitutes. Allowing informal capital and aggregating the two goods
with a finite elasticity of substitution could change the quantitative
response. Those extensions should be assessed after separating pensions from
real public services, because the latter distinction is more fundamental.

Finally, the demographic transition is imposed as a shift between two
stationary ratios. A forecast should use the DANE annual path, endogenous labor
force participation, mortality, migration, and cohort-specific retirement.

\section{Conclusions}

The fiscal experiment can be stated in one equation:
$G_t^{SS}=\bar g_o d_t$. Holding social-security spending per older adult fixed,
DANE's projected increase in Colombia's old-age dependency ratio raises the
long-run revenue requirement by 4.65 percent of initial GDP in the illustrative
calibration.

That demographic pressure does not have a unique effect on capital or
informality. Under the broad-tax benchmark, stationary capital and informality
do not change, although private consumption falls. When half of the additional
outlay is financed from formal payroll, capital falls 13.2 percent and
informality rises 5.9 percentage points. With a 75 percent payroll share,
capital falls 22.9 percent and informality rises 10.2 percentage points. Full
marginal payroll financing has no interior stationary equilibrium.

The model's strongest conclusion is therefore about financing, not the exact
size of the simulated effects. Aging raises the fiscal claim mechanically;
placing that claim on a tax base that contracts with its own rate amplifies the
adjustment through informality and capital accumulation.

The next step should be structural rather than cosmetic: separate pension
transfers from health and care consumption, introduce working and retired
households, feed the annual DANE demographic path into the transition, and
replace the broad benchmark with observed Colombian taxes and a debt rule.
Those additions would turn the present mechanism exercise into a model of
intergenerational social security.

\section{Numerical algorithm}

The code is written in R and uses only base packages. The stationary and
transition calculations proceed as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Given the exogenous populations, compute $d_t=N_t^o/N_t^y$ and
$G_t^{SS}=\bar g_o d_t$.

\item Given $(K_t,G_t^{SS})$ and required payroll revenue, solve
\eqref{eq:formal_choice} jointly with
\eqref{eq:payroll_finance}. The algorithm searches all admissible roots and
selects the root connected to the preceding allocation.

\item Compute formal and informal output, factor prices, tax revenue, and the
government budget residual.

\item In a stationary equilibrium, impose
\eqref{eq:ss_return} and solve the remaining scalar equation for formal
employment. Compute consumption from
\[
  C^\star=Y^\star-G^{SS,\star}-\delta K^\star.
\]

\item For the transition, combine \eqref{eq:euler} and
\eqref{eq:capital} into a two-dimensional map for $(K_t,C_t)$. Capital at the
announcement date is inherited from the benchmark.

\item Evaluate the Jacobian of that map at the final stationary equilibrium.
The calibration has one stable and one unstable root. Select the eigenvector
associated with the stable root.

\item Start close to the final equilibrium on the stable eigenvector and apply
the inverse Euler and resource maps. Continue backward until the path reaches
the inherited capital stock. A scalar root selects the terminal displacement
that reproduces initial capital.

\item Reverse the resulting sequence and append the final stationary
allocation after the terminal discrepancy is below the numerical tolerance.
The reported tests verify the household Euler equation, the resource
constraint, the government budget, the identity $G_t^{SS}=\bar g_o d_t$, the
benchmark targets, and terminal
convergence.
\end{enumerate}

The stable-path method avoids the numerical instability of forward shooting.
It does not smooth the endogenous variables after they are computed. The
transition paths are generated by the model equations themselves.

\section{Alternative preference specifications}

The additive specification in \eqref{eq:utility} can be replaced by a
nonseparable private--public consumption aggregator:
\begin{equation}
  X_t=
  \left[
    \omega^{1/\eta}C_t^{(\eta-1)/\eta}
    +(1-\omega)^{1/\eta}(G_t^{SS})^{(\eta-1)/\eta}
  \right]^{\eta/(\eta-1)}.
  \label{eq:public_private_ces}
\end{equation}
Utility would then be $u(X_t)$. Social-security services change the marginal
utility of private consumption and can affect saving during a transition.
For cash pensions, however, utility should depend on recipients' private
consumption rather than on aggregate outlays. The CES model is economically
different from assigning a positive value to
$\chi_G$ in \eqref{eq:utility}; it should be calibrated as a separate
experiment.
