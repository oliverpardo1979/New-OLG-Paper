\begin{abstract}\small\sloppy
\noindent This paper studies how pension architecture affects labor informality, capital
accumulation, and fiscal adjustment. I build a two-period overlapping-generations model with
heterogeneous productivity, endogenous formal--informal choice, present bias, funded accounts,
a pay-as-you-go (PAYG) program, and an endogenous consumption tax. The exercise compares a
dual-assignment benchmark---lower-productivity formal workers in funded accounts and
higher-productivity formal workers in PAYG---with pillars: every formal worker contributes
to PAYG below a ceiling and to a funded account above it. When PAYG receipts are approximately
matched, pillars raise informality by 6.2 percentage points, lower capital by 5.4 percent, and
require a 5.3 percentage-point consumption-tax increase. Old-age consumption falls by
10.2 percent and experienced welfare by 1.0 percent in consumption-equivalent terms.
An expanded public pillar produces larger effects but raises PAYG receipts by 79 percent, so
it is not an apples-to-apples comparison. Contribution incidence can matter as much as the
public program's aggregate size.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} pensions; informality; overlapping generations; PAYG;
funded accounts; present bias. \quad \textbf{JEL:} E21; H55; J26; J46.
\end{abstract}\fussy

\section{Introduction}
Pension reform in an economy with a large informal sector has a general-equilibrium trade-off.
Mandatory contributions can protect present-biased workers from insufficient retirement
saving, but they also change the return to formal work. When workers can move across sectors,
the distribution of contributions affects the tax base, aggregate saving, and fiscal closure.

This paper asks what happens when a pension system moves from separate funded and PAYG
assignments to a pillar architecture. In the benchmark, lower-productivity formal workers
are assigned to funded accounts and higher-productivity formal workers to PAYG. Under pillars,
the first tranche of every formal worker's earnings finances PAYG and earnings above a ceiling
finance an individual account. This is a comparison between \emph{architectures}, not a model
in which workers choose between pension regimes. The individual choice is formal versus
informal employment.

The distinction drives the result. Funded contributions earn the market return; PAYG benefits
follow a replacement rule and the growth of the contribution base. Extending the PAYG tranche
to workers near the sectoral margin reduces their value of formality. Some become informal,
funded saving and capital fall, and a narrower tax base requires a higher consumption tax.

The main exercise approximately holds PAYG scale fixed: receipts are within 1.2 percent of the
benchmark. Informality rises from 56.4 to 62.5 percent, capital falls 5.4 percent, and the
consumption tax rises from 17.0 to 22.3 percent. An expanded-pillar exercise yields larger
effects but also raises PAYG receipts 79 percent; it therefore combines architecture and scale.

The contribution is threefold. First, the exercise isolates contribution incidence across
heterogeneous workers. Second, it integrates informality, present-biased saving, funded and
PAYG pensions, and fiscal closure. Third, it separates decision utility from experienced
welfare. The appendix gives the numerical algorithm.

\section{Related literature}
The framework follows the OLG approach to social security in \citet{Diamond1977}. Mandatory
retirement programs can address undersaving \citep{Feldstein1985}; present-biased preferences
provide a behavioral rationale \citep{Laibson1998,Imrohoroglu2003}. \citet{Pardo2023} studies
mandatory saving with an informal labor market. Here the question is how funded and PAYG
contributions are allocated across formal workers.

Social insurance cannot be evaluated independently of informality. Payroll-financed benefits
alter formal employment incentives \citep{Levy2008}, while informality reflects worker and
firm choices \citep{Ulyssea2018}. Search and human-capital accumulation shape sector
transitions \citep{Bobba2021}; the shadow economy changes optimal redistribution
\citep{DoligalskiRojas2023}. Pension models with informality include \citet{Joubert2015} and
\citet{McKiernan2020}. Related work studies retirement and occupational behavior when formal
contributions can be avoided \citep{Ferreira2020,Moreno2022}. This paper focuses on which
workers bear the low-return pension tranche and how close they are to the informality margin.

\section{Model}
\subsection{Demography, types, and preferences}
One period is approximately 40 years. Population grows at rate $n$, productivity at $g$, and
an agent survives to old age with probability $1-m$. Type $i\in[0,1]$ has density
\begin{equation}
f(i)=\frac{2.4450}{1.006341403957648}
\exp\!\left[-\left(\frac{i-0.4655}{0.2329}\right)^2\right],
\qquad \int_0^1f(i)\dd i=1.
\end{equation}
Sector-specific efficiency is
\begin{equation}
z_f(i)=\mathrm{base}_f e^{\rho_f i},\qquad z_s(i)=\mathrm{base}_s e^{\rho_s i}.
\end{equation}
With $u(c)=(c^{1-\sigma}-1)/(1-\sigma)$, decision and experienced utilities are
\begin{align}
V(i)&=u(c_1(i))+\beta(1-m)u(c_2(i)),&
W(i)&=u(c_1(i))+(1-m)u(c_2(i)).
\end{align}
Thus $\beta<1$ distorts behavior but is not a normative welfare weight.

\subsection{Technology, households, and sector choice}
Formal and informal output are
\[
Y_f=A_fK^\alpha N_f^{1-\alpha},\qquad Y_s=A_sN_s.
\]
Competitive pre-tax prices satisfy
\[
\widetilde r+\delta=\alpha A_f(N_f/K)^{1-\alpha},\quad
\widetilde w_f=(1-\alpha)A_f(K/N_f)^\alpha,\quad \widetilde w_s=A_s.
\]
Taxes map these into the net return $r$ and effective wages $w_f,w_s$.

For sector $j\in\{f,s\}$ and voluntary saving $a_j(i)\geq0$,
\begin{align}
(1+\tau_c)c_{1j}(i)+a_j(i)&=y_{1j}(i),\\
c_{2j}(i)&=\frac{1+r}{1+g}a_j(i)+p_j(i).
\end{align}
Formal income is $y_{1f}=(1-\tau_\ell-\tau_p)w_fz_f(i)+T_{1f}$ and informal income is
$y_{1s}=w_sz_s(i)+T_{1s}$. Baseline transfers are zero. After solving saving in each sector,
\[
d_f(i)=\1\{V_f(i)\geq V_s(i)\}.
\]
The formal set is computed point by point rather than imposed as an interval.

\subsection{Pension architectures}
In the \emph{dual-assignment benchmark}, $D(i)=\1\{i\geq\bar i\}$ and
\begin{equation}
p_f^D(i)=[1-D(i)]\frac{(1+r)\tau_pw_fz_f(i)}{1+g}
+D(i)\frac{(1-m)b\,w_fz_f(i)}{1+g}.
\label{eq:dual}
\end{equation}
Lower types are funded and higher types PAYG. Assignment is exogenous; workers do not choose
their pension regime.

Under \emph{pillars}, every formal worker's first earnings tranche is PAYG and the excess is
funded:
\begin{equation}
p_f^P(i)=\frac{(1-m)b\min\{w_fz_f(i),\bar y\}}{1+g}
+\frac{(1+r)\tau_p\max\{w_fz_f(i)-\bar y,0\}}{1+g}.
\label{eq:pillars}
\end{equation}
Pillars therefore shift PAYG incidence from high types to the lower earnings of all formal
workers.

\subsection{Aggregation and fiscal closure}
\begin{align}
N_f&=\int d_f(i)z_f(i)f(i)\dd i,&
N_s&=\int[1-d_f(i)]z_s(i)f(i)\dd i,\\
I&=\int[1-d_f(i)]f(i)\dd i,&
K'&=\frac{\int[a_j(i)+a^p(i)]f(i)\dd i}{(1+n)(1+g)}.
\end{align}
Here $a^p$ is funded pension saving. With PAYG receipts $R^{pg}$ and benefits $B^{pg}$,
\[
\tau_c\mathcal C+\tau_\ell w_fN_f+\tau_nw_fN_f+\tau_k\widetilde rK+R^{pg}
=G+\mathcal T+B^{pg}.
\]
The consumption tax closes the budget. Equilibrium combines household optimality, sector
choice, competitive prices, market clearing, the capital fixed point, and public balance.

\section{Conclusion}
Pension architecture changes the formal-employment margin even with an approximately fixed
PAYG envelope. Moving to pillars shifts the PAYG tranche toward lower-earning formal workers,
reduces funded saving and capital, narrows the formal tax base, and forces a higher consumption
tax. In the comparable-scale experiment, informality rises 6.2 percentage points and
experienced welfare falls about 1 percent in consumption-equivalent terms.

These results identify a mechanism, not a universal empirical claim. Pension assignment is
exogenous, employment histories are absent, and the transition is diagnostic. A policy
evaluation should estimate sector transitions, contribution density, benefit eligibility,
administrative costs, heterogeneous mortality, and actual legal budget rules.

\section{Numerical algorithm}
\subsection{Stationary equilibrium}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Build and normalize the type grid and quadrature weights.
\item Guess capital $K$ and consumption tax $\tau_c$ and compute factor prices.
\item For every type and sector, solve the constrained one-dimensional saving problem and
calculate benefits under \eqref{eq:dual} or \eqref{eq:pillars}.
\item Compare decision utilities point by point and aggregate labor, informality, consumption,
saving, and PAYG flows.
\item Update capital from aggregate saving and $\tau_c$ from the public-budget residual, using
damping when necessary. Iterate to tolerance.
\end{enumerate}
The reported steady states use $Q=501$ and maximum residual below $10^{-7}$. A single formal
cutoff is not imposed because pension kinks can create multiple crossings.

\subsection{Matched PAYG and transition}
For every candidate ceiling, the complete pillar equilibrium is recomputed. The outer objective
is $h(\bar y)=R^{pg,P}(\bar y)-R^{pg,D}$. Grid kinks imply an approximate match:
$\bar y=.336$ is within 1.2 percent.

For the transition, guess paths $\{K_t,\tau_{c,t}\}$, solve household and sector choices at
each date, construct implied capital and tax paths, and update with damping. The diagnostic
uses $Q=101$, ten periods, and 334 iterations; its terminal capital gap is
$2.1\times10^{-4}$. Checks cover positive consumption, nonnegative saving, density
normalization, capital consistency, and government balance.
